Abstract
Large language models can write clear and convincing text, but they often state false things while sounding completely sure of themselves. This problem is called hallucination. In critical areas like healthcare, law, and finance, these mistakes can cause serious real world harm. Researchers have tried to tackle this in different ways like looking inside the model, measuring uncertainty and entropy, tracking attention patterns, pulling in facts from outside sources, and fine-tuning the model. However, most of these methods rely on isolated signals and are built separately. Because of this, current systems have a major gap: they might spot an error, but they fail to connect finding the mistake with fixing it and explaining it clearly to the user .In this paper, we conduct a systematic review of 40 research studies in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. We organize the existing literature into a six-pillar taxonomy and compare practical trade-offs, including computational requirements, additional latency, and constraints across both white-box and black-box (API-based) models. Based on this analysis, we identify five major research gaps. To address these limitations, we propose TRUEGUARD, a conceptual framework grounded in closed-loop control principles. TRUEGUARD combines multiple hallucination-detection signals, applies tiered closed-loop corrections when errors are detected, and provides understandable explanations to help users calibrate their trust in the model's output.
Keywords: Hallucination Detection; Large Language Models; Uncertainty Quantification; Explainable AI; Trust worthy AI; Survey; Literature Review
Introduction
Over the last several years, Large Language Models have transformed not only our perceptions of natural language processes but also our comprehension of the process itself. Models based on systems such as GPT 4, LLaMA and Mistral can now compose legal memos, summarize medical records, teach calculus to students, and even write the code of production in challenged code   all things, until recently, strictly in the exclusive domain of human domain experts [3], [18]. The adoption rate has been breathtaking; indeed, almost every type of industry is playing around with some kind of LLM integration. However, as everyone who has worked with these models recognizes a  critical vulnerability persists: large language models frequently generate false information. The technical word is hallucination, which simply means that the model produces text that sounds fluent and confident, but is factually false [7], [18]. As illustrated in Figure 1, these errors generally show up in two distinct ways: intrinsic self-contradictions (where the model disagrees with itself) and extrinsic fabrications (where it invents false facts). Because these two errors look completely different internally, each requires its own detection strategy.
Figure 1: Semantic Entropy excels at detecting extrinsic hallucinations, while attention-based methods better capture intrinsic contradictions.
A model could quote a paper which does not exist, fabricate a historical incident or make a bold lie about a drug interaction. What is so dangerous about that is that even the content that is hallucinated may appear as the normal output to an untrained eye [19]. In the medical, legal, or financial domain where it is applied in high stakes, this is not merely inconvenient, but can be detrimental [3].A group of researchers has instead looked to introspect into    
                            the model itself, examining the activations of hidden layers and interior representations for indicative symptoms 
                            something has gone amiss during generation [1], [2], [5], [13]. Still others have taken an information theoretical     
                            perspective where they quantify the amount of uncertainty or confusion that the model appears to have on its own 
                             outputs [4], [14], [17]. Another body of evidence has investigated the change in the attention mechanism of the  
                            model as it starts to produce fabrications, as hallucinating models tend to  divert attention away from relevant
                            source context.Other researchers in the meantime are working on the mitigation end, by  training models to be able    
                            to say I don’t know rather than guess, [6], [15], or basing outputs on retrieved documents and hence the model has    
                            something real to lean on [20]. Then there is the human aspect of this: how do we explain to users the way the   
                            model is working, how do we establish the level of trust, which responsible deployment  requires [8], [19]?
All these directions have resulted in some useful outcomes. But here is the problem we keep facing. They all have developed to a greater or lesser extent independently. The entropy based detection group is not so much discussing with the retrieval augmented mitigation group. Explainability researchers are building transparent AI frameworks, but these frameworks are not connected to the uncertainty indicators that detection systems already have. Calibration techniques educate models to be candid about their confidence, but no one has examined the possibility that users differ in their belief in their calibrated outputs. The sphere is fruitful; however, it is disjunctive, and disjuncture is crippling practical development.
In this paper, we bridge this gap through a systematic review of 40 representative publications that cover key developments in hallucination detection, evaluation, mitigation, and explainability. Rather than just listing these papers, we organize them into a structured taxonomy to show how they connect, where they differ, and what they require to work in practice. Specifically, this paper makes the following contributions:
Six-Pillar Taxonomy: We organize the 40 studies into a systematic six-pillar taxonomy, making it clear how different research methods tackle specific parts of the hallucination problem.
Comparative Trade-off Analysis: We evaluate practical trade-offs across these pillars, focusing on computing costs, response latency, and the real-world differences between open-access (white-box) models and commercial API (black-box) models.
Five Persistent Research Gaps: We identify five structural gaps that remain unsolved in the literature: reliance on single isolated signals, high computing costs, the disconnect between detection and mitigation, superficial explanations, and the lack of calibration to human trust.
Control-Theoretic Architecture (TRUEGUARD): Using principles from closed-loop control theory, we introduce TRUEGUARD, a conceptual framework that unifies multi-signal detection, tiered corrective feedback, and user-facing explanations to help people properly calibrate their trust.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section II details our PRISMA-guided review methodology and search protocol. Section III presents our six-pillar taxonomy and discusses each category in detail. Section IV conducts a comparative analysis across pillars, evaluating operational trade-offs and deployment constraints. Section V specifies five persistent research gaps. Section VI introduces the TRUEGUARD conceptual closed-loop control architecture. Section VII outlines key future research directions, and Section VIII concludes the paper.
METHODOLOGY AND LITERATURE SEARCH
 To ensure this review is transparent, rigorous, and repeatable, we conducted our search following the      
 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.This  
 section outlines the databases that we searched, the query we used, our inclusion and exclusion criteria, 
 the screening process, our quality appraisal protocol, data extraction variables and the organization of the 
 final 40-paper corpus.
2.1 Databases and Search Strategy
 We scanned five academic and preprint repositories, both for computer science papers and for system engineering venues:
                  1. Scopus: Indexes peer-reviewed computer science and engineering journals, including Taylor & Francis publications such as Systems Science & Control Engineering, alongside IEEE and ACM proceedings.
                  2. Web of Science (Core Collection): Covers important papers in systems science, automation and cybernetics.
                  3. IEEE xplore Digital Library: Collection includes IEEE transactions and conference proceedings in neural systems, control, and machine learning.
                  4. ACM Digital Library: Indexes computing surveys, algorithmic frameworks and evaluation benchmarks.
                   5. arXiv.org: Covers recent preprints, capturing the latest trends and developments in large language models.
The search period ranged from January 1, 2020 until February 28, 2026. The end date was selected As the fixed date to freeze literature corpus in order to make it comparable.
We used the following search query across paper titles, abstracts, and author keywords:
("Large Language Models" OR "LLM") AND ("hallucination" OR "factual error") AND ("detection" OR 
"mitigation" OR "uncertainty" OR "attention" OR "internal states" OR "explainability")
2.2 Eligibility Criteria
Clear inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined prior to screening to select technically sound and reproducible studies.
1. Inclusion Criteria(IC)
•IC1: Papers that focus directly on the study of hallucination detection, uncertainty estimation, explain ability and mitigation in LLMs.
•IC2: Papers that present clear mathematical formulations, algorithmic models or empirical evaluations on public benchmarks.
•IC3: Papers tested on autoregressive transformer models (LLaMA, GPT, Mistral, or Falcon).
•IC4: Articles from peer-reviewed journals, conference proceedings, or widely recognized technical preprints.
•IC5: Manuscripts written in English.
•IC6: Studies connecting LLM reliability to systems and control concepts (such as state monitoring, uncertainty quantification, fault detection, and closed-loop corrective feedback), aligning with the scope of Systems Science & Control Engineering.
2. Exclusion Criteria (EC):
•EC1: General natural language processing studies (such as basic translation or sentiment analysis) that do not evaluate hallucinations or truthfulness.
•EC2: Editorials, short opinion pieces, commercial announcements or perspective notes, but no empirical testing.
•EC3: Papers that are not sufficiently detailed in architecture and algorithm for technical reproducibility assessment.
•EC4: Duplicate papers or preliminary workshop extended abstracts superseded by full journal or conference versions.
2.3 Screening Process	
                         The selection process followed the four standard PRISMA stages:
Identification: Database queries returned 688 initial records across the five sources: arXiv (214), Scopus (186), IEEE Xplore (112), Web of Science (92), and ACM Digital Library (84). Removing 196 duplicate records across databases left 492 unique records.
Screening: The two authors independently reviewed the titles and abstracts of the 492 unique records against IC1–IC6 and EC1–EC4. This step removed 324 off-topic records (218 general NLP or off-topic papers, and 106 non-technical notes or demos), leaving 168 candidate articles for full-text examination. Any screening discrepancies between the authors were resolved through discussion and consensus.
Eligibility: All 168 candidate articles were read and evaluated in full. A total of 128 articles were excluded with documented reasons: prompt heuristics only without internal probing (54 papers), lack of standard baseline comparisons (42 papers), and redundant methodology (32 papers).
Included: A final corpus of 40 representative studies met all eligibility and quality criteria and was selected for synthesis. 2.4 Quality Appraisal Protocol
We evaluated each eligible study across four technical criteria:
Methodological Rigor: Does the paper provide explicit mathematical formulas, loss functions, or clear algorithmic steps?
Benchmark Validity: Was the method evaluated on standard public benchmarks (such as TruthfulQA, HaluEval, HELM, or TriviaQA)?
Metric Reliability: Does the study report standard evaluation metrics (such as AUROC, F1-score, accuracy, or Brier score) instead of uncalibrated scores?
Deployment Transparency: Does the paper state its access requirements, distinguishing between white-box access (requiring access to model weights and internal activations) and black-box access (working only through generated text and commercial APIs)?
2.5 Data Extraction Protocol
For each of the 40 selected papers, both authors extracted the following variables:
Model family and scale (e.g., LLaMA-2/3 7B–70B, GPT-3.5/4, Mistral-7B).
Signal source (hidden representations, attention weights, token output probabilities, semantic entropy, or external knowledge retrieval).
Operational access type (white-box, grey-box, or black-box).
Resource overhead (number of forward passes, inference latency, and extra GPU memory needed).
Control components (real-time error detection mechanisms, confidence thresholds, and corrective feedback pathways).

2.6 Corpus Organization: Core Studies vs. Foundational Frameworks
The 40 included studies are organized into two groups:
20 Core Architectural Studies: Recent technical papers providing comparative data on internal layer probing, token entropy, attention head monitoring, distribution shift detection, and calibrated reinforcement learning.
20 Foundational Benchmarks and Mitigation Frameworks: Landmark benchmarks (e.g., TruthfulQA, HaluEval), evaluation metrics (e.g., FActScore), mitigation methods (e.g., RAG, Self-RAG, CoVe), and black-box sampling baselines (e.g., SelfCheckGPT) that establish operational standards in the field.
Taxonomy and Literature Review
The 40 reviewed papers are organized into six pillars: (1) Internal State-Based Detection, (2) Uncertainty Quantification and Entropy Methods, (3) Attention-Based Detection, (4) Evaluation and Benchmarking, (5) Mitigation through Calibration and Retrieval, and (6) Explainability and Human Trust.
Boundary Crossings and Taxonomy Justification:
Some methods naturally cross the boundaries between pillars. For example, Semantic Entropy Probes (SEPs) [14] use internal hidden states (Pillar 1) to predict semantic entropy (Pillar 2) in a single pass. Similarly, LLM-Check [12] combines attention patterns (Pillar 3) with internal vectors (Pillar 1) for better accuracy. We classify each paper based on its main signal source and where it acts in the model's generation process.
Pillar 1: Internal State Based Detection
The intuition of this pillar is easy to understand. Any model that is hallucinating should manifest this in some part of the inner processing of the model. The model being what it is, knows what it knows and what it is making up, the question is whether we can read those signals. These ways along this line work in six out of our forty paper reviews.
MIND: Su et al. [1] had a clear cut idea: they would not check the outputs of LLM in post processing (which is slow and costly) but observe the internal states of the model itself when it is being generated and find hallucinations in real time. MIND is the name of their framework which does just this. It imparts the presence of inference as it uses these computation representations that are already underway, which is why they are not significantly more or less burdensome to the forward flatten than the focus images. The most attractive feature of MIND is its capability in learning without the aid of any human inferred labels of the hallucinations it sees, which makes it even more appealing. The other contribution made by the authors is the introduction of a benchmark by the name HELM, which bundles together the various model outputs together with the internal states it contains, providing a testbed to other researchers. What is more significant than it may initially appear is the unsupervised aspect. Scale dependent hallucinations are costly and more subjective and so any technique that averts them has a practical advantage.
PRISM: The disadvantage of having one headache containing supervised internal state detectors is that it is overfit to the domain on which it is trained. A medical QA detector will do pathetically on legal text. Zhang et al. [2] goes at this directly with PRISM, which employs particularly structured prompts to bias the model internalization in such a way that truthfulness related structure becomes more salient and, more importantly, more robust across disciplines. The crucial assumption behind this is that the shape of the truthfulness of a model states of hidden reasons is really garage general  it simply does not always manifest itself so obviously without a kind of coercion. It is born out in their experiments in a variety of fields: PRISM provides a significant boost to the cross domain detectors.
MHAD: Zhang et al. [5] are zoomed in. Instead of accessing all the hidden states, they make use of linear probing to determine how much each neuron is more sensitive to hallucination in each of the layers. The most important fact in their results is somewhat shocking: the spread of hallucination awareness is not uniform through the process of generation. Rather it is focused on two particular lines: the very beginning and the very end of generation. Through the extraction of the outputs of the identified neurons at these two points only and concatenating them into a small single size vector, MHAD can attain a high detection and not have to perform an entire sequence analysis. It is a good use of feature selection as a means to win over brute force methods.
Semantic Entropy Probes: Kossen et al. [14] focus on a bottleneck at hand. One of the uncertainty measures that work better in hallucination detection is semantic entropy but to make it work correctly would involve generating 5 to 10 additional output sequences per query and cluster them using meaning. That is a lot of extra compute. Semantic Entropy Probes (SEPs) have the insight that the hidden states of the model already have sufficient information to estimate semantic entropy, you just need to be trained to extract it. SEPs are easy to train classifiers which, given the hidden state of one generation, are used to give predictions based on the hidden state of the next generation. They do not even use the multi sample step. Their ablation work has an interesting fact, in that particular token positions and particular layers are more likely to have this information than others, seeming to imply a structured and complex system of uncertainty representation within the model.
HALLUSHIFT: Dasgupta et al. [13] assume a temporal perspective. Their argument is that hallucinations do not occur immediately, but they creep in. A model can begin on a good factual base and get progressively derailed into fiction less a story teller can begin correctly and slowly begin to add to it progressively. HALLUSHIFT compares the statistical distribution between the hidden states in one generation to the next generation, trying to detect changes that have been obtained to represent such a factual drift. The comparison to human thought is intentional, and, we believe, correct. When either humans or machines enter a state of hallucination, they frequently seem to be holding on to their superficial coherence, but the veracity of the ground becomes lost.
HALLUSHIFT+ Given the title, Nath et al. [16] continue the thought of HALLUSHIFT and develop it into the multimodal domain. A new form of model appears when images and text are processed in the models.
hallucination happens: the model can talk about things that are not represented in an image or do not describe relationships between spaces in a false way. HALLUSHIFT++ learns abnormalities in the layer wise dynamics of Multimodal LLMs, beyond distributional shifts, analyzing assumptions in individual layers. A cross agency benefits to the closeout consideration One advantage, one worthwhile to note, is that since this approach considers the internals of the model as opposed to it being an external LLM evaluator, there is no circularity problem of one potentially hallucinatory model to judge another.
What this pillar tells us. Together, all six articles start a counterargument that hallucination remains more than an output level problem, that it puts fingerprints on the inside of the model. These days we have unsupervised techniques [1], cross domain techniques [2], tools robust at the granularity of an entire neuron [5], effective ap  approximations [14], temporal monitors [13], and techniques with multiple modalities [16]. The primary drawback to which they are all common lies in the requirement of white box access. None of these approaches would be of help, in case you are not able to look inside the model.

 Principles of UQ in LLMs Kang et al. [7] compile what is, to the best of our knowledge, the most comprehensive list of foundations of the application of uncertainty quantification to LLMs of a specific kind. They go through the traditional separation of epistemic uncertainty (the model lacks sufficient information to be certain) and aleatoric uncertainty (the question is actually ambiguous), and then follow the ways these notions have been rethought as applied to the special circumstances of autoregressive language generation. Their division of the methods into confidence based, sampling  based and ensemble based families provides a handy map of it. More productively, perhaps, they are straightforward with the outstanding issues: most UQ approaches are computationally intensive, have sub optimal inter domain transfer, and make unwell calibrated confidence estimates.
Semantic Energy: This is a subtlety which Ma et al. [4] have observed: normal semantic entropy involves probabilities which have already been run through the softmax tragedy, which in fact conceals how uncertain the model is. Two actually quite different logit distributions can give similar looking probability distributions after softmax. Semantic Energy is an algorithm that attempts directly to do what the penultimate layer specifies by optimizing a Boltzmann based energy formu  lation of what the model thinks to be true will happen before probability balancing brings smoothness to things. The consequence is a set of uncertainty estimates that are more knowledgeable of true model confusion especially in such instances where semantic entropy is producing the meaningfully hiss of confidently read indications.
Entropy Production Rate: The vast majority of approaches we have discussed up, until now, require that you have a look inside the model. Moslonka et al. [17] are not lucky enough to do that. They are aiming at the more widespread than ever situation where you are.invoking a commercial API   say, that of OpenAI or Anthropic   that provides the generated text, and possibly the highest few log probabilities per token, but no more. Under these restrictive conditions they obtain an Entropy Production Rate in the non greedy decoding that can be performed with the available log probabilities. The remarkable feature of this is the one shot design: you can have an estimate of the hallucination of one generation, without necessarily re querying the API a few times. This is a significant contribution to a production environment with API calls that can be bought at a cost and generated with a latency delay.
SelfCheckGPT: Manakul et al. [21] introduced SelfCheckGPT for black-box models accessed through commercial APIs, where model weights and internal activations are unavailable. The underlying principle is that when a model has genuine factual knowledge, multiple responses generated with random sampling will remain consistent with each other. Conversely, when the model hallucinates, independently sampled answers will contradict one another. SelfCheckGPT evaluates this consistency across companion outputs using sentence similarity, question answering, and natural language inference (NLI). On the WikiBio dataset, this approach achieved an AUROC of 0.90 to 0.93 without requiring parameter access. The primary drawback is increased computational latency and cost, since generating multiple companion outputs for each query requires extra inference passes and API tokens.
What this pillar tells us. These studies demonstrate that measuring uncertainty is an effective way to detect hallucinations. However, an operational trade-off exists between model access and computational cost. White-box methods (such as Semantic Energy and SEPs) provide accurate uncertainty estimates in a single forward pass, but require direct access to model weights. Black-box methods (such as SelfCheckGPT and EPR) function through standard APIs, but either require generating multiple companion outputs or rely on limited token probabilities. Production systems need uncertainty methods that maintain reliable detection while minimizing inference delay.
Pillar 3: Attention Based Detection
At their core, transformers rely on attention mechanisms: every token produced is a weighted blend of earlier tokens. When a model starts to hallucinate, these attention weights shift in noticeable ways, allowing researchers to spot errors in real time. Figure 4 highlights this with LLM-Check, showing that its black-box version runs up to 450 times faster than the full white-box version while losing very little detection accuracy. 
Figure 4: LLM-Check's black-box variant achieves a 450x speedup with only a modest drop in AUROC compared to the full white-box setting.
We believe that it is an area that has been slightly overlooked, which has been made by the Map of Misbelief: Hajji et al. [9]. They introduce visibility into the division between intrinsic and extrinsic hallucinations (when the model contradicts itself), and extrinsic hallucinations (when the model simply builds new information with no foundation whatsoever). This is important since the two kinds of hallucination need quite different detection strategies as demonstrated by their experiments. Sampling based methods such as semantic entropy perform well in picking up extrinsics but the intrinsic hallucinations are not well suited by such methods. Inverting it, their attention based aggregation of input tokens proved to be significantly more adapted to intrinsic hallucinations. This complementarity is a weakness into which the idea that multi  signal approaches should be necessary and not just nice to have can be utilized.
RAUQ: The authors of RAUQ [11] never identified themselves, finding in the anonymous text something particular and practical: some attention heads in the transformer that they refer to as uncertainty conscious heads act differently when the model is hallucinating. In particular, these heads deemphasize past context tokens to a near observer that the model is turning its back to the evidence. RAUQ recognizes these heads automatically and collapses their behavior into a repetitive scoring scheme giving rise to a sequence level uncertainty estimation. The entire process supplements on a single forward pass and they evaluate it on twelve tasks and four LLMs. That analysis has a very broad spectrum and it is encouraging; it indicates the potential that this is not a construction of a specific model or data set.
LLM Check: The approach of Sriramanan et al. [12] is closer to kitchen sink, which proves to be remarkably effective. They consider the hidden states, attention maps and output probabilities simultaneously and they are tested in white box and black box scenarios. The efficiency is what is outstanding:
They multiply their approaches by traveling up to 450 times faster than standard foundations and in fact better identification accuracy. It is huge to any person who is considering using hallucination detection in a system where latency is important. They also consider detection in Retrieval Augmented Generation systems, which are becoming a reality as RAG is becoming the default system of knowledge intensive applications.
What this pillar tells us. Hallucination analysis is a poorly exploited field using attention patterns. The greatest lesson is the result of complements by Hajji et al. [9]: various types of hallucinations leave various traces, and no single signal can capture those. It is a powerful driving factor of fusion based strategies.

TrustLLM: Huang et al. [3] considered trustworthiness being a multi-dimensional issue. They do not analyze hallucination but consider the eight dimensions of truthfulness, safety, fairness, robustness, privacy, machine ethics, transparency, and accountability when assessing LLCM. They have experimented with sixteen mainstream models on over thirty datasets and some of their results are worth consideration. Reliability and brute strength also move in a coercive manner more reliable models are usually more appreciable, and it is positive. There is still a narrowing of the gap in proprietary models in most trust dimensions. And with a warning: excessive vigor in safety filtering may indeed have a backfiring effect in that the model seemed to cause the rejection of rightful requests, which should undermine the trust that it aims to establish.
Faithfulness Evaluation: Jing et al. [10] base the evaluation problem on industrial reality. As they worked on travel domain data at Expedia, they created templates of rubrics to use LLMs as judges of faithfulness, where generated content is produced and graded on one of several measured scales. Also, they addressed the data issue itself by coming up with techniques to create synthetic unfaithful data, which can subsequently be used to train or fine tune NLI models. Their experimental work reveals that GPT 4 makes surprisingly accurate and better explained judgments of faithfulness and experimental exploitation of NLI models fine tuned on the synthetic data to display actual improvements in performance. It is applicable practice and the discipline requires additional practice.

Comprehensive Hallucination Survey: Alansari and Luq  man [18] formed a compilation which can be called a field guide in the research of hallucinations. They follow the entire lifecycle through the collection practices with data that bring about biases, architectural interventions that place them in blind spots, and inference time behavior that causes hallucination. They offer a good indexing system to anyone attempting to traverse the burgeoning literature because their taxonomies of detection meth  ods and mitigation strategies are helpful. Whereas our review is concerned with pinpointing gaps and synthesis in the methods, theirs gives a comprehensive list of what has previously been done.
TruthfulQA: Lin et al. [22] created TruthfulQA to test whether language models repeat common human myths and misconceptions. The benchmark has 817 questions covering 38 topics, including medicine, law, finance, and politics. The questions are specifically designed to trick models into giving false answers based on popular beliefs. A key finding was that bigger models were often less truthful than smaller ones. Because larger models read more web data, they picked up common human falsehoods more effectively. TruthfulQA is now a standard tool for testing model honesty.
FActScore: Min et al. [23] created FActScore to check factual accuracy in long paragraphs, such as biographies. In long responses, a single sentence often mixes true facts with made-up details. Labeling an entire sentence as simply "true" or "false" is inaccurate. FActScore fixes this by breaking down long text into individual atomic facts—simple statements like "person X was born in year Y." It then checks each piece of information separately against sources like Wikipedia. This produces a clear percentage of true facts, ensuring that half-true sentences do not hide factual errors.
What this pillar tells us. Benchmarks are improving, but testing remains fragmented. Broad evaluations [3] and atomic fact metrics [23] give good diagnostic data. However, the field still lacks a single benchmark that tests the whole loop together: finding an error, correcting it, and explaining it clearly to the user. Pillar 5: Mitigation Through Calibration and Retrieval Augmentation
Half the war is the diagnosis of hallucinations. The other half is taking action against them   preferably before the user can hear the bad output. The entropy spike detection based on RL calibration: One extended abstract [6] suggests the marriage of two concepts, which tend to be on different papers. The first concept is entropy spike detection: when generating, calculate the entropy of the output distribution at every token and take an alarm over any sudden changes (by scaling by z scores) as indicators of there being an unstable point in the distribution. The second concept is self-regulated  confounding: condition the model to evaluate its own confidence and contrast the self doctrine withactual accuracy. The ingenious thing is in the mixture of these. The RL incentive equally punishes undeserved certainty and entropy spikes therefore the model learns to be well calibrated and constant in its reasoning chains. Fine  grained shaping provides increased texture to the training signal, since most RLHF techniques utilize only a scalar reward.
Behaviorally Calibrated Reinforcement Learning: Wu. et al. have an eye catching premise in that hallucination is not a bug in the conventional meaning. Predictability of outcome of training is what rewards being right ish as compared to being honest. A model trained to yield the highest possible probability of sampling up in the correct looking text learns to make swamping guesses, even a tiny likelihood of happening to be appropriate makes the guessing of the form of an act more pleasurable than lack of understanding. This is aggravated by the usual form of RL when the rewards are binary (right/wrong) leading to what the authors aptly term as good test  takers instead of honest communicators. Their solution: condition the model to logically reason and produce calibrated probabilities based on strictly proper scoring legislation, and condition the model to act in ways that it really does not know.

The most notable finding is that, as they demonstrate, calibration is a transferable meta skill, a smaller model with calibration adequately can do significantly better on updating about uncertainty than a significantly larger frontier model. It is one of the findings that are practical in nature. As shown in Figure 8, BehCal RL achieves the lowest calibration error across three benchmarks while keeping overall task accuracy high. ExpandR: Yao et al. [20] approach the problem in the perspective of retrieval side. Retrieval augmented generation is that concept: prior to the generation of an answer, one should seek out some relevant documents and serve as grounding. However, on most RAG systems the retriever and generator are attached by way of a bolt, but they are not very well synchronized. ExpandR approaches this problem differently, optimizing the two together. The LLM is conditioned to generate query expansions with which the retriever can realistically work on and the retriever is conditioned to retrieve documents which the LLM can actually use. They do this joint optimization using Direct Preference Optimization, whose reward function is to compromise quality of retrievals with consistency of generation. The strategy achieves the 5% boost of retrieval, and in a competitive discipline, it is considerable Figure 7 shows that ExpandR's joint optimization consistently beats standard standalone retrieval baselines across four separate benchmarks.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG): Lewis et al. [25] proposed RAG to give language models access to outside reference files. Instead of relying only on its own memory, the model looks up related documents from a database and uses them to write its answer. This prevents errors when the model lacks facts about a topic. A key drawback is that normal RAG searches on every question, even when the model already knows the right answer or when the retrieved files are noisy.
Self-RAG: To make retrieval more selective, Asai et al. [26] designed Self-RAG. Rather than looking up documents for every prompt, the model uses special learned tokens to control its own behavior. It outputs a retrieval token only when it is unsure of a fact, and a critique token to check if the retrieved text is actually helpful. This cuts down on unnecessary database searches and improves accuracy compared to standard RAG.
Chain-of-Verification (CoVe): Dhuliawala et al. [24] introduced Chain-of-Verification (CoVe) to catch and fix mistakes after text is generated. It does this without retraining the model. The method uses four steps: first, it writes an initial response; second, it generates fact-checking questions about its own claims; third, it answers those questions on their own to prevent bias; and fourth, it writes an updated final response. Across several QA benchmarks, this process lowered error rates across different model sizes.
What this pillar tells us. Mitigating hallucinations is not just about catching mistakes after text is written. It also means improving how models search, reason, and handle uncertainty. Frameworks like Self-RAG [26] and ExpandR [20] show that search tools and language models work better when built together. At the same time, calibration methods [15] help models learn when to answer and when to admit they do not know. Ultimately, users need to trust model outputs, which requires both technical accuracy and clear explanations. People need to know when and why an answer can be trusted. Figure 9 shows how explanations affect user trust, demonstrating that clear explanations help users judge answers much better when comparing outputs side by side.
Figure 9: Impact of explanation framing on human user trust across isolated and comparative presentation modalities.
XAI of LLMs: Herrera [8] steps out of methodological details and poses this question: what is the meaning of it even when an LLM explanation is deemed to be good? It will depend on the questioner. Herrera pinpoints four dimensions of concern, namely; faithfulness (is the explanation what the model did?), truthfulness (is the explanation factually accurate?), plausibility (is the explanation understandable to a human?), and contrastivity (is the explanation why the model made this choice and not that one). These dimensions do not necessarily coincide. An ideal honest explanation of the 96 head attention mechanism of a transformer would be honest but quite impossible to the majority of users. One may say a simplification which is rather an unfaithful representation of the process of the model. These tensions, Herrera alludes to, are the main design problem facing XAI in the LLM age.
Human Trust in LLM Responses: Sharma et al. [19] conducted human experiments that seek to determine the actual impact of explanations on trust which has disconcerting implication of its findings. Adding explanations to bet ter responses did actually increase trust in model responses when the participants were presented with multiple responses of the model side by side  people discriminated using the explanations. Good. However, once the participants were asked to assess the responses individually (which is the reality of most real life situations), the trust advantage disappeared completely. There was similar evidence of people believing trusting confident re  responds, with or without explanations of it and with or without the correctness of the responses. Differently put, explanations enable individuals to make comparisons and not evaluate. To system designers, it implies that you must create explanations but not only explanations you must create the interaction to facilitate meaningful comparison.
What this pillar tells us. The difference between generated values by detection systems (numbers) and those required by users (under  understanding) is huge. There is a technical possibility of computing grounded explanations,  attention maps, entropy values, distribution shift etc are all phenomena understandable to users, but the integrations of no  body ever made these signals available to explainable systems. And even after they do, interaction design will be of equal importance in comparison with the contents of the explanation [19].
Comparative Analysis
Having walked through each pillar separately, it is possible to draw back and see the cross cutting patterns. There are various themes that are repeated in the literature in a manner that should be discussed.
The Complementarity Argument.
When it comes to making one or another finding out of this review, we would like to highlight this: various detection signals spot various types of hallucinations. It most clearly was demonstrated by Hajji et al. [9], when attention proves to be effective in intrinsic hallucinations and entropy in extrinsic ones, but the trend is even broader. Representational anomalies are picked up by internal state probes [1], [5], [14] and distribution shift trackers [13], [16], temporal drift by entropy monitors [4] and low confidence outputs flagged by entropy monitors [4]. None of them can claim the entire spectrum.
The Efficiency Question
There is a constant conflict between the detection ability of the method of hallucination and its cost of operation. Semantic entropy insiders receive good numbers with the addition of 5 to 10 additional generation cycles per query [14]. SEPs and RAUQ deal with single  pass detectors, but sacrifice a little precision. According to LLM  Check [12], this trade off is not also absolute: through heinous engineering, one can achieve 450x speedups without sacrificing quality. The larger implication that involves the SEPs result [14] that semantic entropy is already being encoded in hidden states on the first pass is that the corresponding information can already be present in a single pass, it is just that we have to more effectively discover how to retrieve it.
The Access Problem
One would then want to be forthright about this: most of the best techniques require looking within the model. They need white box access to internal state probes, attention analysis, logit level entropy, and all others [1], [2], [4], [5], [9], [11], [13], [14]. Only EPR [17] andparts of LLM Check [12] are good when you are calling an API. Because most deployed in the real world experience proprietary models available solely via API, it is a major weakness in the literature.
Advertisement and Prevention: Ships in the Night.
Among these things that stood out to us as we went through these papers is the fact that there is little connectivity between detection research and mitigation research. The detection papers [1], [2], [4], Scores become hallucinating as in [5], [9], [11]  [14], [17] and they do not mention what the next step must be. The mitigation papers [6], [15], [20] modify the model behavior without being fed into any detection system. Another exception is the entropy spike work [6], which makes use of the detection signals within the reward function, and Wu et al. [15] also come to the same result based on the calibration side. However, overall, there is more the two communities can do to communicate.

Identified Research Gaps
Let us distill the preceding analysis into five concrete gaps.
Signal Isolation
There is no one who is adding more than 2 detection signals although there is no doubt strong evidence of various signals detecting various things [9], [11], [12]. In principle, a fusion mechanism which is trained to weight internal states, attention, entropy and distributional dynamics with respect to the context, would do better than any single method. The difficulty is whether to engineer such a fusion in a manner that is not merely parameter additive but makes good use of the compliments.
Computational Overhead
Using a combination of multiple signals increases cost. Nevertheless, the outcome of the SEPs [14]   that hidden states encode semantic entropy indicates that many signals may be recoverable of the same calculation. In cases where internal states, attention maps, and logit distributions can be obtained during a single forward pass, it may be significantly less expensive to extract multiple signals than run several methods separately.
Mitigation Disconnection connected with Detection
Problems are indicated by detection systems. Mitigation systems cure behavior. However, they are not connected. What would it be supposed ? Should high uncertainty detection invoke selective retrieval to the uncertain claims, but not to retrieval augmentation in general? Or would crowdsourcing be improved by having some models that are dynamically directed to alternating confident generation one alternatively with pursuing calibrated abstinence based on real time detection feedback? This closed loop architecture has simply not been conspicuously shown in literature at the moment.
Explainability Deficit
The detection systems are aware of how it was that they flagged it, because of an entropy spike, or a shift in attention, or a distri  butional anomaly. Nevertheless, they fail to inform the user of that. Filling this gap refers to the translation of technical signals into the explanations that should meet the four XAI dimensions Herrera [8] established, faithful, truthful, plausible, and contrastive.
Trust Calibration Gap
Sharma et al. [19] demonstrated that the same explanations, despite having them as such, relate to trust depending on their presentation even though their influence is significant. There is no current system where trust communication is planned on such empirical findings. The outcome is the disconnection between the output of the systems (confidence scores) and the experience of the users (opaque numbers without calibration).
6.Trueguard
A Closed-Loop Control Architecture To resolve the five research gaps identified in Section 5, we propose TRUEGUARD (Trustworthy, Unified, Real-time, Explainable Guard). Rather than treating hallucination detection and correction as disjoint post-processing steps, TRUEGUARD models autoregressive text generation as a closed-loop control system. The architecture continuously monitors internal model dynamics, detects factual faults using calibrated decision thresholds, executes targeted corrective actions in real time, and provides transparent explanations to help users properly calibrate their trust.
6.1 Multi-Signal Sensor Layer: 
Real-Time Model Monitoring Existing detection techniques typically examine a single indicator in isolation. TRUEGUARD unifies four complementary signal sources extracted during a single forward pass without introducing substantial computational delay:
Internal State Probing (State Sensor): Probes attached to hidden layers monitor internal representations to catch factual errors before words are generated, extending MIND [1], PRISM [2], MHAD [5], and Semantic Entropy Probes [14].
Attention Head Monitoring (Observation Sensor): Tracks whether attention heads remain focused on input evidence or wander off into unsupported generation, adapting the Map of Misbelief [9] and RAUQ [11].
Logit Energy and Entropy Monitoring (Output Sensor): Measures token probability fluctuations, entropy spikes, and logit energy to catch sudden moments of model confusion, building on Semantic Energy [4], Entropy Spike analysis [6], and Entropy Production Rate [17].
Distribution Drift Tracking (Temporal Sensor): Compares layer activations across consecutive tokens to detect gradual factual drift over long generation sequences, based on HALLUSHIFT [13] and HALLUSHIFT++ [16].
6.2 Signal Fusion and Conflict Resolution :
Different signals excel at catching different types of hallucinations.
For example, attention tracking reliably captures internal self-contradictions, whereas logit entropy and internal state anomalies better identify fabricated external entities. Drawing from multi-signal insights established by LLM-Check [12], TRUEGUARD combines these four indicators through an adaptive weighting layer. This fusion produces a unified real-time Risk Score (ranging from 0 for fully grounded text to 1 for high risk), preventing false alarms caused by temporary noise in any single sensor.
6.3 Fault Detection Logic and Decision Thresholds:
 The aggregate Risk Score is continuously compared against two calibrated operating thresholds:
Normal Operating Regime (Risk Score below Warning Threshold): Generation is stable, grounded, and consistent. Text is streamed directly to the user with standard confidence indicators.
Warning Operating Regime (Risk Score between Warning and Fault Thresholds): Mild uncertainty or weak contextual grounding is detected. The system triggers Tier 1 corrective feedback.
Fault Operating Regime (Risk Score exceeds Fault Threshold): High uncertainty or a severe factual error is identified. The system enforces Tier 2 external retrieval or Tier 3 abstention.
6.4 Tiered Closed-Loop Corrective Actions :
When the fault detection logic identifies an error, TRUEGUARD applies proportional corrective actions across three stages:
Tier 1: Local Constrained Re-sampling: For minor uncertainty, the model rolls back generation by a few tokens and re-samples using constrained decoding and temperature scaling, preventing compounding errors in the style of Chain-of-Verification [24].
Tier 2: Targeted Grounding Retrieval: When an error stems from missing knowledge, TRUEGUARD queries an external reference source (following ExpandR [20], standard RAG [25], and Self-RAG [26]) specifically for the unverified claim, avoiding the latency overhead of re-generating the entire response.
Tier 3: Calibrated Abstention: If retrieval cannot resolve model uncertainty below the fault threshold, the system engages behavioral calibration [15] to honestly state its factual limitations ("I do not have sufficient verified evidence to answer this accurately") rather than generating a hallucination.
6.5 Human-Centered Explainability and Trust Calibration ;
In parallel with closed-loop correction, TRUEGUARD converts internal sensor measurements into plain-language explanations structured around the four XAI dimensions identified by Herrera [8]:
Token-Level Risk Markers: Directly highlights risky words or phrases in the generated text.
Signal Attribution: Informs the user why an alarm was triggered (e.g., an entropy spike, lost attention to context, or hidden representation shift).
Contrastive Rationales: Outlines the grounded facts the model considered versus the unverified claims it discarded.
Comparative Formatting: Uses side-by-side presentation layouts to facilitate human verification, aligning with Sharma et al. [19], TrustLLM [3], and Jing et al. [10] so that users can accurately calibrate their trust in the system.
7. Future Research Directions
We wrap up the substantive section of this review by pointing out what we believe should be closely considered in the future. A primary challenge is the implementation of numerous signals in a single architecture. The immediate open question is which the best method of combining heterogeneous hallucination signals is. Learned weighting, information theoretic gating, and task conditional routing represent plausible directions,empirical comparisons between types of hallucinations and domains of tasks would assist the community in achieving an agreement on practical design principles. 
Multimodal hallucination. It has been demonstrated by HALLUSHIFT++ [16] that the internal state analysis can also be applied to the vision language models, but only the tip of the iceberg has been explored. Such issues as cross modal attention, visual grounding, and modality specific uncertainty should be researched in multi signal detection.
Online calibration. Wu et al. [15] have shown that calibration is transferred between tasks, whereas real deployments experience changing distributions as time goes by. The issue of the way, or how, to ensure quality of calibration in non-stationary settings, say, online learning, or periodical re calibration, is an open question. Personalized trust. Individuals vary in how they react to model description and confidence signals [19]. Individual user profiles to the style of explanation, abstention thresholds, and confidence presentation would move forward the vision of stakeholder aware AI, as enacted by Herrera [8]. Safety guarantees. Introducing empirical methods of UQ the survey of which was conducted by Kang et al. [7] paired with formal verification methods may bring the field closer to being probably safe rather than being provably safe which should be the case of the most demanding deployment settings. Privacy preserving detection. Distributing detection systems among institutions that do not share the sensitive model internals would be an appropriate response to the privacy aspect of TrustLLM [3]. Federated learning strategies appear quite appropriate here since they have not yet been pursued in the present context. Scaling to very large models. Solutions like SEPs [14] and MHAD [5] can work well with 7B13B models. However, the probing and feature selection strategies perhaps require reconsideration on 70B and above parameter models when a small increment in overhead can be significant. Closing the black box gap. If there exists a quality difference between black box and white box detection there will be a significant percentage of real world deployments that will not receive the necessary attention. Taking EPR style [17] techniques one step further, or devising still novel methods to glean hallucination signals out of fixed API outputs, is a viable priority.
8.Conclusion
The field of LLM reliability is moving rapidly and taking on new dimensions. The forty papers we have considered span internal state analysis [1], [2], [5], [13], [14], [16], [27], [28], uncertainty quantification [4], [7], [17], [21], attention-based detection [9], [11], [12], evaluation and benchmarking [3], [10], [18], [22], [23], [29], [35]–[37], calibration and mitigation [6], [15], [20], [24]–[26], [30]–[34], [38]–[40], and explainability and trust [8], [19]. Each of these studies has contributed valuable insights to our understanding of hallucination. What was made evident during this review is the extent to which these efforts are complementary: attention captures intrinsic inconsistencies, entropy detects extrinsic fabrications, internal states catch semantic divergence, and mitigation techniques operate without diagnostic feedback. 
We introduced TRUEGUARD as a conceptual architecture grounded in closed-loop control principles to bridge these separate efforts: multi-signal sensor fusion to achieve robust detection, fault-detection decision thresholds to govern intervention, tiered closed-loop actuation to achieve active correction, and grounded explanations to calibrate human trust. Hallucination cannot be resolved through any single isolated technique; it requires bridging detection, mitigation, and human cognition. Through this review, we hope to make those vital connections clear.
